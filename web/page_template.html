StereoNet, from scratch

Two photographs taken a few centimetres apart go in; a 3D scene you can turn in your hands comes out. The network, its backpropagation and its training were written in plain Python with zero imports. This page runs the trained weights in your browser.

What runs when you press the button

1

Scale

A quick pass guesses how far objects shift between the eyes, then corrects itself, so the pair runs at a size the network can search.

2

Match

Shared-weight features from both images are compared at __DMAX__ candidate shifts. A good match costs nearly nothing.

3

Decide

3D convolutions clean the costs; a soft-argmin turns them into sub-pixel disparity.

4

Sharpen

Two refinement stages, guided by the photo itself, restore edges the coarse match blurred.

5

Refine

At full resolution, only the 21 disparities around the network's answer are re-checked, with census matching and semi-global smoothing in 8 directions. Both views must agree. Flat colour segments are snapped to planes.

6

Build

Depth = focal length × baseline ÷ disparity. A mesh with a vertex every second pixel carries the photograph as its texture, and opens from the left camera's own viewpoint. Behind foreground edges, a darker layer continues the background the cameras could not see.

Example pairs from the Middlebury stereo datasets (Scharstein & Szeliski, 2003; Scharstein & Pal, 2007), used with their ground truth to score each result on this page. None of them was used in training. Without calibration, geometry is correct up to an overall scale. For honesty: a classical census + SGM matcher on its own, when told each scene's true depth range, scores 0.80 px on the same eight test scenes, better per pixel than this pipeline. Here the network finds that range by itself and keeps far, flat and hidden areas sane, which gives lower surface-normal and point-cloud (Chamfer) error.
